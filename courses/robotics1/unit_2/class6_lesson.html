Part 1  ·  Team Roles & Responsibilities (15 min)

Engineering Teams Are Not Just Groups

In Unit 1 you learned to design purposefully — every decision on your robot had a reason. Unit 2 adds a new layer: project management. Even a brilliant design fails when the team doesn't know who is building what, who makes the final call, or who is tracking the schedule.

Professional engineering teams assign roles. A role is not a task you do once — it is an ongoing responsibility you own for the life of a project. Roles create accountability: when something goes wrong, the team knows exactly who owns the fix. When a decision needs to be made, there's no argument about who decides.

Four roles cover every project in this course. Each role has a defined scope. All four roles contribute to the build, but each person owns one area. Roles rotate between projects so every student builds every skill.

📋 Project Manager
  • Owns the schedule and Gantt chart
  • Runs team check-ins and sets the agenda
  • Tracks progress against milestones
  • Escalates blockers before they become crises
  • Makes the call when the team is stuck on a non-technical decision
🔧 Lead Engineer
  • Makes final technical and design decisions
  • Reviews all sketches for feasibility before any building
  • Runs the decision matrix and selects the concept
  • Delegates build tasks by skill and availability
  • Reviews the module at each build milestone for quality
🔨 Builder / Fabricator
  • Responsible for build quality and fit
  • Reports interface issues to the Lead Engineer immediately
  • Maintains tools and keeps the workspace organized
  • Tests physical connections before final assembly
  • Notifies PM if build is running behind timeline
📝 Documenter / Analyst
  • Maintains the engineering notebook in real time
  • Records test data, iteration decisions, and rationale
  • Owns the risk register (introduced in Class 8)
  • Logs all trial times and score data during testing
  • Prepares the post-project summary for assessment

Part 2  ·  Role in Action — I Do / You Do (18 min)

Who Owns This Decision?

Knowing the roles is not enough — you need to know how to use them under pressure. During a build day, decisions come fast. Parts don't fit. Someone is behind. The driver wants to try something different. Without role clarity, the team stops and debates. With role clarity, each person knows exactly what to do next.

The pattern is simple: technical decisions go to the Lead Engineer. Schedule and priority decisions go to the Project Manager. Build quality decisions go to the Builder. Documentation decisions go to the Documenter. When a decision crosses roles, the two owners talk — and the Lead Engineer has the final word on anything involving the design.

I Do Watch the teacher model role thinking through three scenarios

Scenario A — The collector module keeps falling off the robot during teleop testing.

Lead Engineer: "This is a design issue — I need to look at the mount point. I'll decide whether we change the bracket angle or add a second screw. I'll sketch the fix and confirm with the Builder before we change anything."

Project Manager: "How long does that fix take? I need to update the Gantt. If it's more than 10 minutes, our second trial might not happen today."

Documenter: "I'm logging this: module detached at T+38s, Trial 1. Cause suspected: single M3 bolt at torque limit. Lead Engineer is redesigning the mount. I'll note the retest time when it happens."

Notice: Everyone responded in their role. No one person did everything. The team kept moving because each person knew their lane.

Scenario B — The team has 8 minutes left and two tasks unfinished.

Project Manager decides which task to finish and which to defer — this is a scheduling call, not a technical one.

Scenario C — The Documenter notices the notebook is three entries behind.

Documenter flags this to the PM. They agree the Documenter pauses build support to catch up. PM adjusts the task assignment — this is their call, not the Lead Engineer's.

Part 3  ·  Team Charter & Norms (16 min)

The Agreement You Make Before Things Go Wrong

A team charter is a written agreement about how your team operates — not just what you will build. It defines communication norms, decision-making protocols, and what happens when someone doesn't meet their commitments. In professional engineering, teams revisit the charter at the start of every project.

The charter is not a punishment document. It is a protection document. When a conflict arises during the build, the team has already agreed on the process for resolving it. This removes personal conflict from technical disagreements — the charter makes it about process, not personality.

The most important section is the decision-making protocol: who makes which calls, and when. Without this, every decision becomes a negotiation — and during a 74-minute class with a build to finish, that time is not available.

I Do Watch the teacher model one charter section — decision-making protocol

Writing the decision-making protocol out loud:

"Technical decisions — which design, which parts, which attachment method — belong to the Lead Engineer. They have final say. But they must explain the reasoning before deciding, not after, so the team understands and can support it.

Schedule decisions — what to work on, when to pause, what to defer — belong to the Project Manager. They check in with the Lead Engineer on technical feasibility before shuffling tasks.

If the PM and Lead Engineer disagree about whether to keep building or pause to fix a problem, we use a 90-second discussion rule: each person states their position once with a reason. If no agreement, the Lead Engineer decides during a build, the PM decides on scheduling. No further debate."

Why this works: It pre-defines every conflict category before any conflict happens. The team signed this — so when it comes up, there's no argument about the process.

Part 4  ·  MiniBot Mini Challenge — First Concepts (20 min)

Three Concepts Before You Touch a Single Part

The Unit 2 Challenge: Your team will design, build, test, and iterate a collector module that attaches to your Unit 1 REV MiniBot. Using teleop mode, your driver will collect and return FTC game objects — 4-inch foam cubes and 4-inch rubber balls — to a scoring zone within a set time window. The team with the most game objects scored wins.

Constraints: The module must use REV hardware (channels, brackets, screws, servos, or DC motors from your kit). It must attach and detach cleanly using bolts — no permanent modifications to the MiniBot from Unit 1. It cannot extend more than 6 inches beyond the robot footprint in any direction. The robot must still drive normally with the module attached.

Today you generate three concept sketches. In Class 7 you will evaluate all three using a weighted decision matrix. In Class 8 you will build, test, and iterate. Every idea — even a bad one — is worth sketching, because your third idea is almost always better than your first.

I Do Watch the teacher model how to generate — and evaluate — a concept

Concept A — Passive scoop/funnel (teacher sketches live): A wedge-shaped channel bolted to the front of the robot. As the robot drives forward, cubes slide up the ramp and into a bucket. No moving parts, no motors. Advantage: simple, low part count, low failure risk. Trade-off: only works if the robot drives at the object precisely. Cannot pick up balls from the side or reach objects in corners.

Key questions I ask about every concept before committing to it:

1. Does it work for cubes? For balls? (Does it handle both game objects?)
2. How many REV parts does it need — can I build this in one class period?
3. What is the single most likely failure mode?
4. Does it violate any constraint? (Size limit, attachment method, drive clearance?)

Notice: I ask these questions before sketching details. Answering "does it violate constraints?" at the concept stage saves 30 minutes of wasted building later.
