Team Roles & Norms 8 terms
Role
Class 6

An ongoing responsibility you own for the life of a project — not a one-time job. Roles create accountability: when something goes wrong, the team knows exactly who owns the fix.

Contrast: A task is a one-time job ("attach this bracket"). A role is a standing responsibility ("own the schedule for the whole project") — that's the Project Manager's role, not just a task.
Accountability
Class 6

Knowing exactly who owns the fix when something goes wrong. Roles exist to create accountability — without defined roles, problems get noticed but no one is responsible for solving them.

Project Manager
Class 6

The team role that owns the schedule and Gantt chart, runs check-ins, and makes the call on non-technical decisions like task priority. Has final say on scheduling questions.

Lead Engineer
Class 6

The team role with final say on all technical and design decisions — which parts to use, how to attach them, and what the design specification requires.

Builder / Fabricator
Class 6

The team role responsible for build quality and fit. Reports interface problems to the Lead Engineer immediately and flags the Project Manager if the build is running behind schedule.

Documenter / Analyst
Class 6

The team role that maintains the engineering notebook in real time, records test data and iteration decisions, and logs trial times and scores. Owns the risk register (Class 8).

Team charter
Class 6

A written agreement about how a team operates — covering communication norms, decision-making, and accountability. Drafted before any work begins so conflict has a defined resolution process.

Key idea: A charter is a protection document, not a punishment document. It removes personal conflict from technical disagreements by deciding the process in advance.
Decision-making protocol
Class 6

The section of a team charter that defines who makes which type of decision and what happens when team members disagree. The single most important section of the charter.

Example: "Technical decisions belong to the Lead Engineer. Schedule decisions belong to the Project Manager. If they disagree, each states a reason once with a 90-second limit, then the Lead Engineer decides technical questions."
Project Planning 11 terms
Project management
Class 6

A system for organizing who does what and who decides what on an engineering team. Even a brilliant design fails when the team doesn't know who is building which parts or who makes the final call.

Concept sketch
Class 6

A drawing with annotations made before any building begins, showing one possible approach to a design problem. Teams generate three genuinely different concept sketches before selecting one — your first idea is usually the most obvious, not the most effective.

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)
Class 7

A breakdown of a project into phases, then tasks, then subtasks — until every piece of work is small enough that one person can own it, estimate it, and complete it.

Gantt chart
Class 7

A timeline that puts each task from a WBS into a visual schedule so the whole team can see what is happening now, what is next, and what is at risk of running late.

Dependency
Class 7

A relationship where Task B cannot start until Task A is finished. You can't attach a collector mechanism before the mount bracket is built — that's a dependency.

Critical path
Class 7

The chain of dependent tasks that determines how long a project takes. If any task on the critical path slips, the whole project is late. It matters more than the total number of tasks.

Float
Class 7

The amount of time a task not on the critical path can slip without delaying the project's end date. Tasks with float give the team breathing room; tasks without it don't.

Don't confuse with buffer: Float is a mathematical property of the schedule — you discover it, you don't add it. Buffer is a deliberate planning choice.
Milestone
Class 7

A specific, verifiable checkpoint — not a task. Either the condition is met or it isn't. "Module attached to MiniBot and robot drives normally" is a milestone, not a task you can be partway through.

Buffer
Class 7

Extra time added to task estimates — typically 10%, planned in advance — to absorb unexpected problems without delaying the milestone. A dropped screw or a wrong-size bracket each cost a few minutes; buffer accounts for this.

Weighted decision matrix
Class 7

A tool that removes personal preference from concept selection. Criteria are set, a weight is assigned to each based on importance — before scoring — then each concept is scored against every criterion. The highest weighted total wins.

Override rule: The Lead Engineer may choose a concept that didn't win the matrix, but must document specific reasoning in the notebook before the build begins. Changing weights after seeing scores is bias, not engineering.
Design specification
Class 7

A written contract for the build: what the module must do, every constraint it must satisfy, and a complete parts list. A good spec means the Builder can start assembling without asking any questions — any question asked during the build is a spec gap.

Risk & Iteration 9 terms
Risk
Class 8

Anything that might prevent a project from succeeding. Not all risks are equally dangerous — some are very likely but low impact; others are unlikely but catastrophic.

Risk register
Class 8

A living document that lists every identified risk, scores it on likelihood and impact, calculates a priority score, and assigns a mitigation strategy. Updated in real time as risks materialize during the project.

Likelihood
Class 8

A score from 1 to 3 measuring how likely a risk is to happen. Combined with impact to calculate a priority score (likelihood × impact).

Impact
Class 8

A score from 1 to 3 measuring how serious a risk would be if it happened. Combined with likelihood to calculate a priority score (likelihood × impact).

Priority score
Class 8

Likelihood multiplied by impact (L × I). Scores of 1–3 are "Monitor," 4–6 are "Significant," and 7–9 are "Critical." Higher-priority risks need a mitigation strategy before the build begins.

Mitigation strategy
Class 8

A specific action taken before a risk happens to reduce its likelihood or impact. "Hope it doesn't happen" is not a mitigation strategy — a real one is a deliberate, purposeful choice.

Hypothesis
Class 8

A testable prediction that connects test data to a proposed design change. "The intake is misaligned, causing left-edge misses" is a hypothesis — it predicts a specific cause that a change can target.

Iteration
Class 8

A deliberate, data-driven change made to improve performance between trials — not a random fix. A hypothesis-driven iteration connects Trial 1 data to a specific predicted mechanism, so Trial 2 can confirm or deny it.

Why it matters even when scores don't improve: A denied hypothesis is still useful data — it rules out one explanation and narrows down where the real cause might be.
Status update
Class 8

A written summary using a traffic-light system (🟢 on track, 🟡 at risk, 🔴 blocked) that communicates progress, blockers, and next steps to the team and teacher. A Red status is not failure — it's honest engineering.

Team Communication 1 term
Stand-up meeting
Class 8

A short, structured check-in (3 minutes) where each team member answers: what I completed, what I'm working on now, and any blocker. Blockers are noted, not debated — they go on a follow-up list.

Mini Challenge Terms 11 terms
Control
Challenge

A game element is being held, gripped, carried, pushed, or otherwise directly manipulated by a robot's collector module — not merely touching the robot by accident.

Game element
Challenge

A 4-inch foam cube or 4-inch rubber ball used in the Mini Challenge. Scored identically regardless of type.

Common area
Challenge

The shared region of the playing field where all game elements are prepositioned before a match and are accessible to both robots.

Scoring depot
Challenge

The team-specific destination location where a delivered game element must come to rest to count for scoring.

Match
Challenge

A single 90-second, teleop-only contest between exactly two teams. There is no autonomous period in the MiniBot Mini Challenge.

Single Delivery
Challenge

Delivering exactly one game element to a team's scoring depot in one scoring action. Worth 1 point.

Double Delivery
Challenge

Delivering exactly two game elements to a team's scoring depot at the same time, in one scoring action. Worth 3 points.

Continuous Control Bonus
Challenge

A bonus earned when a robot maintains uninterrupted control of a game element from collection to delivery. Worth 2 points, earned in addition to the delivery points — not instead of them.

Excess Control Violation
Challenge

Controlling 3 or more game elements at once, where the excess persists 3+ continuous seconds, 3+ cumulative seconds across multiple segments, or shows no effort to relinquish. A Minor Penalty — 5 points to the opponent.

Round Robin
Challenge

A tournament format in which every team plays a match against every other team. No team sits out more matches than any other.

Challenge Winner
Challenge

The team with the highest total accumulated points across all of its matches in the round robin — determined by total points, not total match wins.

Why this matters: A team that wins fewer matches but scores more total points across the tournament can still win the overall challenge.
