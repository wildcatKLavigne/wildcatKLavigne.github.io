Part 1  ·  What Is Risk Management? (10 min)

Seeing Problems Before They Happen

A risk is anything that might prevent your project from succeeding. Not all risks are equally dangerous. Some are very likely but low impact. Others are unlikely but catastrophic. A risk register is a living document that lists every identified risk, scores it on likelihood (1–3) and impact (1–3), calculates a priority score (likelihood × impact), and assigns a mitigation strategy.

A mitigation strategy is what you do before the risk happens to reduce its likelihood or impact. "Hope it doesn't happen" is not a mitigation strategy. "Pre-drill a second mounting hole so we can swap the bracket angle in under 2 minutes if the primary mount fails" is a mitigation strategy.

Risk management is not pessimism — it is purposeful design (Unit 1, Class 1) applied to the project itself. You can only manage risks you have named first.

I Do Watch the teacher model identifying and scoring risks for the collector module

How I identify risks: "I ask: 'What could prevent us from finishing the build, attaching it correctly, or getting a clean trial?' Design risk: the spec missed a dimension and the module doesn't fit. Parts risk: a bracket size isn't in our stockpile. Build risk: the mount isn't secure and the module detaches mid-trial. Test risk: the driver hasn't practiced and fumbles the controls during Trial 1."

Scoring and prioritizing: "Likelihood 1 (unlikely) to 3 (very likely). Impact 1 (inconvenient) to 3 (project-threatening). Priority = L × I. The module-detaching risk: Likelihood=2 (single-bolt mounts have failed before in this class), Impact=3 (zero score for that trial). Priority = 6 — significant. Mitigation: Builder adds a second mounting bolt and tests it by hand before the first trial."

The register is a living document: "When a risk becomes real — the module does detach in Trial 1 — the Documenter updates the register: 'Risk materialized Trial 1, T+22s. Mitigation implemented: second bolt added. Result: held through Trial 2.' This becomes part of the iteration log too."

Part 3  ·  Trial 1 & First Iteration (17 min)

Test, Then Change On Purpose

An iteration is a deliberate change made to improve performance, based on test data — not a random fix. You have a hypothesis ("if we widen the intake by 2 cm, balls will enter more reliably"), you make the change, and you test again to confirm or deny the hypothesis. If you can't explain why a change worked, you can't repeat it next time.

I Do Watch the teacher model writing a hypothesis-driven iteration

Bad iteration (no hypothesis): "The claw didn't grab the ball well, so we just made the opening bigger." This might work, but the team won't know why — if it fails, they have no data to learn from.

Good iteration (hypothesis-driven): "Trial 1 showed the claw closed before fully surrounding the ball — it grabbed 1 of 3 attempts. Hypothesis: the claw's closing angle is too narrow for the ball's 4-inch diameter. Change: widen the claw's resting angle by 15°. Predicted result: grip success rate increases because the ball has more room to seat before the claw closes."

Why this matters: "After Trial 2, we either confirm (grip rate went up — angle was the cause) or deny (grip rate stayed the same — something else is the cause, keep investigating) the hypothesis. Either way, we learned something we can write down and reuse."

Part 5  ·  Status Update, Stand-Up & Gantt Revision (15 min)

Communicate, Then Update the Plan

The most common reason engineering teams fail isn't bad engineering — it's communication. A part is missing and everyone assumes someone else is handling it. A problem isn't mentioned until it's too late to fix. Two tools prevent this: the stand-up meeting (a 3-minute check-in) and the status update (a written summary using a traffic-light system: 🟢 on track, 🟡 at risk, 🔴 blocked). A Red status is not failure — it's honest engineering. What fails teams is a Red status nobody reports until it's too late.

I Do Teacher models a bad vs. a good status update

Bad (vague, no action, no accountability): "We finished the module. It mostly worked. We might try to improve it more."

Good (specific, action items, owner): "Status: 🟡 Yellow. Module attached and functional by 45 min. Trial 1 scored 3 cubes, 0 balls — claw angle too narrow for balls. Mitigation: widened claw angle 15° (Iteration 1). Trial 2 scored 3 cubes, 2 balls — hypothesis confirmed. Remaining risk: mount uses a single bolt, scored 'monitor' in risk register — recommend a second bolt before the official challenge run. Owner: Builder."

Why specificity matters: "The good update tells the reader everything they need without follow-up questions — respecting everyone's time and demonstrating professionalism."
